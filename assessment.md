Q1 — Where the agent made me faster

The agent wrote api/resale.js and api/health.js in 15 minutes from one prompt, including the response.ok check before the body read, the median computation and the Cache-Control header. Written myself, I estimate 1 hour, and almost none of that would have been typing: the bottleneck is looking things up, specifically the shape of the CKAN datastore reply, the correct filters encoding, and what a serverless handler signature looks like on Vercel. I spent the time I got back on prompting creating information, which is the part of the weekend that was mine rather than the machine's. This was work I could have written but only slowly, not work I could not have written at all, and the difference matters: the agent compressed lookup time, not judgement. The reverse case was the Vercel environment settings / the front-end state styling, where three exchanges of prompting were slower than doing it in the dashboard, and my log shows me stopping and doing it by hand at flase in api/health.

Q2 — Where it cost me time

What I saw was a page of my own HTML with a 200 when I opened /api/resale. It took 1 hours. The real cause was he api folder sitting inside src so Vercel never ran it. My verdict is that this was an unfinished instruction : at the moment I sent that prompt I had decided the back error , so there was nothing for it to be faithful to. The remedy is deciding before prompting, not a better model.

Q3 — Something that looked right and was not

What I accepted: the health panel the agent produced, which I kept because it rendered a clean three-card grid and reported the upstream status I had asked for. Why it read as correct: it showed a green shield, the words "not required" against the credential, and a status line reading "200 OK". How long it survived: 1 hours. What exposed it: reading the JSX rather than the screen, and finding that the status line is built by concatenating the string "OK" onto whatever number arrives, and that the shield icon is hard-coded emerald regardless of what keyConfigured says. A 503 from my own guard would render as "503 OK" beside a green tick. The failure is not the code being complicated; it is that I judged a diagnostic by how it looked, which is the exact thing a diagnostic exists to stop me doing. If the 2017 sort or the string prices also bit you, add the same four beats here. If they did not bite you because you were warned about them in advance, say so plainly and say where the warning came from.

Q4 — What I had to know to supervise it

I could only catch the "200 OK" because I knew that an HTTP status is a number with a separate reason phrase, so a status of 429 does not carry the word OK, and a panel that always prints it is printing a decoration rather than a reading. The harder half: what I would have needed to know to catch what I did not catch. My function sends filters and sort to the CKAN datastore, and I never independently established that those parameters are honoured on this host. If they are silently ignored, my screen shows January 2017 records with a correct-looking median and nothing anywhere reports an error. To catch that I would have had to know that a query parameter a server does not recognise is discarded without complaint, and then go and check the month field against a calendar. That is a piece of knowledge about how APIs fail quietly, not about housing, and it is the kind of thing that stays mine to hold.

Q5 — Decisions I kept, and the ones I should have

In order: that the product is a resale price benchmark for Hbd; that the source would be the data.gov.sg HDB datastore rather than a property portal; Ang Mo Kio and 4-room as the fixed scope; median rather than mean, because resale prices have a long right tail and a mean overstates what my user would actually pay; a 24-hour cache, because HDB registers these monthly and asking hourly asks more often than the source changes; and showing the sample size and reference month beside the figure, so the number is not a bare claim.

Which never reached the list? Two. First, the decision that the user sees a serverless diagnostics panel at all: I asked for a health endpoint, the agent built it into the interface, and I never asked whether a first-time buyer benefits from reading "Upstream Handshake". Second, and more seriously, the four sentences. I decided there would be four states and the agent decided what they say, which is why the refused state tells my user "The data.gov.sg datastore service rejected the request (Status 429)" and the unreachable state prints a raw network error in monospace. Those are sentences written for me, not for someone deciding whether to buy a flat, and neither tells her what to do next. Judged against §8 that is the boundary moving without me noticing: it cost nothing while the service was up, and it is the whole of my product on the morning it is not.

Q6 — Thirty people

I would put the review at the boundary rather than at the end: a Thursday-morning read of what each person's product says when its source is down, before the Friday release, because that is the decision most likely to have been made by an agent and least likely to be caught by testing the happy path. I would refuse to let an agent settle two things alone: what a user is told when the system is failing, and what a number on the screen is allowed to claim about its own freshness. Detecting that it had been settled anyway is the hard part, and I would do it through the prompt log rather than the code review, because a decision I made appears as a sentence and a decision I accepted appears as a silence, and thirty silences in one sprint is a pattern nobody can currently see. The thing my organisation would have to start checking, and does not: whether a green status indicator is reading a real value or printing a constant. My own health panel failed exactly that test this weekend, and I built it.
